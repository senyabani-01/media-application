Are our cities really getting warmer?

Thermometers in 100 major cities have recorded temperatures for over a century. In this story you will see what they say, how sure we can be, and then explore your own questions.

Navigate with the Next/Back buttons or the arrow keys.

1. One line, 110+ years

Each point is one year's average temperature across 100 major cities, compared with the 1901–1930 average (the zero line). Above zero is warmer than that period.

Another view: each coloured stripe is one year, from 1900 (left) to 2012 (right). Blue is cooler, red is warmer.

4. Reading charts responsibly

  • Framing: the vertical axis starts near the data, not at zero. This makes change easier to see, but can look dramatic. Compare with the numbers in the captions.
  • Short windows mislead: try a 10-year window in chapter 3. Long-term trends need long records.
  • Cities only: these are 100 big cities, not the whole planet. Cities can also be warmed by their own growth, and oceans are not included.
  • One dataset: scientists compare many datasets before drawing conclusions.

Source: Berkeley Earth, Climate Change: Earth Surface Temperature Data (Kaggle), major cities file. Years with missing months and cities with gaps were removed; anomalies are calculated against 1901–1930.
